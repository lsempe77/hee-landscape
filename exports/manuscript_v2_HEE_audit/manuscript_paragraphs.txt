0019 Summary 
0020 Background 
0021 Health systems worldwide are facing critical pressures that threaten access to and quality of healthcare, with profound long-term social and economic consequences. In many high-income countries, population ageing linked to rising healthcare costs and fiscal constraints are placing increasing pressure on health system financing and service delivery. Low- and middle-income countries face diverse challenges, including growing demand for healthcare, constrained domestic fiscal resources and, particularly in low-income and aid-dependent countries, reductions in development assistance for health.  
0022 Methods 
0023 We systematically identified and classified global literature on health financing and health economics published after 2010 integrating Large Language Models (LLM) into our mapping workflow at searching, screening of titles and abstracts and data extraction. We consulted with an Advisory Group of stakeholders for setting the scope, developing and publishing the protocol, and refining our analysis and visual presentation of the findings.  
0024 Findings 
0025 Our framework categorizes system-level health financing instruments into recurrent financing functions, supply chain, and capital financing/resource generation, and links these to intermediate and final outcomes.

0094 Introduction 
0095 Countries are being asked to pay for more health care with less outside money. For instance, development assistance for health has been cut 21% from $49.6 billion in 2024 down to $39.1 billion in 2025 (Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation 2025), and the cuts fall hardest on the low- and middle-income countries that depend on it most. In high-income countries, ageing populations and new technologies mean health spending is projected to grow faster than the economy (OECD 2025). Governments in all these settings face the same questions. How should they raise revenue and pool it? Which services should they cover, and how should they pay the providers? How do they get public money to the services that need it? These are the functions of health financing (Kutzin 2001). How well a system performs them affects access to care, financial protection, efficiency and equity (Papanicolas et al. 2022), and ultimately population health (Lastuka et al. 2025). 
0096 Research on these questions is copious but scattered. A PubMed search for "health financing" alone returns hundreds of thousands of records. Reviews have therefore covered one part of the field at a time. Recent examples cover the efficiency of health spending (Brikci 2024), financing strategies for universal health coverage (Hajji et al. 2025), the effectiveness of health financing policies (Oweidat et al. 2026), public financial management (Musiega et al. 2024) and financing in fragile and conflict-affected states (Bertone et al. 2019). No study has mapped the literature as a whole, so basic questions remain open. Which financing functions are studied, and which are neglected? Which health-system objectives do the studies examine? Which countries are studied, and does research follow need and aid? And is the research written by people based in the country studied, or elsewhere? 
0097 Decisions about which services to cover and pay for also need evidence on value for money. Full economic evaluations compare the costs and consequences of different ways of delivering care, and they inform health technology assessment and the design of benefit packages. We therefore mapped two bodies of research published since 2010: studies of health financing functions, and full economic evaluations of health care.  
0098 We drew on recent methods that use large language models to map large bodies of literature, and applied them to the global empirical research on health financing published since 2010. We had two objectives. The first was to map and characterise this literature: we screened more than one million records and coded each included study by financing function, health-system outcome and country, and each economic evaluation by disease and intervention. The resulting map shows where the evidence is concentrated, where it is thin relative to need, and where there is already enough to synthesise. The second was to make the evidence available to policymakers, funders and researchers through an open-access platform. Together, the map and the platform are meant to support learning and inform research priorities. 
0099 This document presents the results of this exercise. The following sections describe the conceptual framework and methods, present and interpret the mapped evidence base, discuss limitations, and outline implications for future research.  
0100 Methods 
0101 We conducted a systematic evidence map with two arms, health financing (HF) and health economic evaluations (HEE), following the principles of systematic mapping set out by James et al. (2016). Large language models screened the records and extracted data, and their decisions were checked against records screened and coded by the review team.  
0102 Similar to earlier maps of large literatures (Berrang-Ford et al. 2021), we used automated methods to make a global map feasible. Unlike them, we used large language models to judge each record against the eligibility criteria, rather than supervised classifiers trained to predict relevance. We report the study selection following the PRISMA 2020 statement (Page et al. 2021). The protocol was registered on OSF (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/2BK8N) and published in Wellcome Open Research (Marion et al. 2026). Appendix A provides full methodological detail, and Section 8 lists deviations from the protocol. 
0103 Scope 
0104 The map’s two arms each following its own framework and own eligibility criteria. All records for training and validating the LLM prompts were double screened against these criteria by human reviewers until the inter-rater agreement reached 85%. We report each arm on its own and never add the two counts together. A study can qualify for both arms. 
0105 Health financing  
0106 The HF arm follows the health financing functions framework first set out by Kutzin (2001) and the World Health Report 2000 (World Health Organization 2000), now embedded in WHO’s Health Financing Progress Matrix (Jowett et al. 2020) and health system performance assessment framework (Papanicolas et al. 2022). It covers revenue raising, pooling, purchasing, benefits decisions (benefit design, coverage and rationing) and public financial management. We add three areas “upstream” of service delivery: capital investment in infrastructure, capital investment in other health-system inputs such as the workforce and medicines (World Health Organization 2000), and the financing of supply chains (Baker et al. 2025). Studies of the financing system as a whole form a ninth category, “overall”. A study is in scope when it examines at least one of these functions in enough depth; reporting a financing outcome, such as out-of-pocket spending, is not enough on its own. These functions affect the objectives of a health system: equity, efficiency, quality, transparency and accountability, health, responsiveness and financial protection, and beyond them economic development (Papanicolas et al. 2022). We record which of these each study examines. Appendix B gives the definitions, developed with health financing experts.  
0107 Health economic evaluations 
0108 The HEE arm follows the framework for the economic evaluation of health care set out by Drummond et al. (2015). A full economic evaluation compares two or more alternatives on both their costs and their consequences. It takes one of four forms: cost-utility analysis (CUA), cost-effectiveness analysis, (CEA), cost-consequence analysis (CCA) or cost-benefit analysis (CBA). Partial evaluations, which examine one alternative only, or costs or consequences only, are out of scope, as are cost-minimization analyses (Appendix A). Economic evaluations show whether a service offers value for money, so they can inform benefits decisions. Most, however, evaluate a single treatment or program rather than a financing decision. Because of this distinct focus and its size, we map this evidence as its own arm. 
0109 Overlaps between HF and HEE arms 
0110 In the HEE arm, only full economic evaluations comparing the costs and clinical outcomes of two or more health interventions or modes of healthcare delivery were included. Other economic analyses, including those that did not meet the HEE criteria as full economic evaluations, were still eligible for the HF arm (i.e., as benefit decisions) as long as they satisfy the full HF eligibility criteria and the financing function classification. This includes partial economic evaluations that the HEE arm would exclude. Similarly, health technology assessments (HTA) that were eligible for the HEE arm were also eligible under benefit decisions if they primarily informed coverage or reimbursement decisions. See more details in Appendix C.  
0111 Search strategy and eligibility criteria 
0112 We searched five databases between 27 July and 3 August 2026: Scopus, the Cochrane Library, and MEDLINE, EconLit and Global Health on the Ovid platform. The search had two strands. The first found records that mentioned health, financing, and at least one financing topic, such as taxation, insurance or provider payment. The second found economic evaluations, using terms adapted from Pitt et al. (2016) and the Canada’s Drug Agency search filter. A record found by either strand was retrieved. We excluded studies of animals only and limited the search to records from 2010 onwards (search strings in Appendix D). After removing duplicates in EPPI-Reviewer, 1,154,050 records remained. This single pool was screened once for each arm. 
0113 Each arm has its own code list (Appendix E). The criteria are applied in order, and the first criterion a record fails decides its exclusion. A record that cannot be judged from its title and abstract moves on to the next stage. Table 1 summarizes the criteria.

0139 Screening 
0140 Reference sets 
0141 The review team screened two sets of records by hand, each for both arms. A development set of 600 records was used to write and revise the screening prompts. A blinded audit of 1,000 records, drawn at random from the corpus, was screened by the team before any model was run and kept apart to measure accuracy. 
0142 We wrote a separate prompt for each arm, setting out its code list as step-by-step instructions with worked examples. We revised each prompt until it met three thresholds set in the protocol: sensitivity of at least 0.95, Cohen’s κ of at least 0.70, and expected calibration error of at most 0.10. We used different models for different tasks, chosen for their accuracy against the human-coded records, their cost at this scale and, where one model checked another, for coming from a different developer (Appendix A). 
0143 Screening procedure  
0144 A large language model, GLM-5.2, screened every record with an abstract. It assessed each record three times, and a record was included if at least two assessments included it. Records the model could not judge moved on to the next stage. Where a record had no abstract, we tried to retrieve one; records still without an abstract were excluded. 
0145 HEE. A second model reassessed every record on which the three assessments disagreed, and the record was included if either model included it. 
0146 HF. Screening was iterative, with the blinded audit used to check each stage. A second pass re-screened the first-pass inclusions to remove false inclusions. The audit then showed that the prompt applied the protocol’s criteria more narrowly than intended: it excluded studies of how a financing mechanism affects health outcomes, which the protocol counts as eligible. We realigned the prompt with the protocol. A third pass re-screened the 39,259 first-pass exclusions most likely to be affected and returned 6,987 records to the next stage. 
0147 Accuracy  
0148 At calibration, both prompts met the protocol thresholds on the development set (HEE: sensitivity 0.962, κ 0.815; HF: sensitivity 0.969, κ 0.852). On the blinded audit, the HEE screening recovered 32 of 37 eligible records (0.86; 95% CI 0.72–0.94). The final HF set recovers 32 of 47 (0.68; 95% CI 0.54–0.80). The revised HF prompt recovers 41 of 47 (0.87) when run on the whole audit, but it was applied only to the targeted third pass. Appendix A3 gives the full results and statistical tests. 
0149 Comparison with other approaches 
0150 Ranking records with EPPI-Reviewer’s classifier, or with conventional supervised classifiers trained on the same human-coded records, would have required reviewers to read 14–56% of records to find 95% of eligible studies. The language model selected about 5%. At equal workload it found more eligible studies than every classifier, although against the best classifiers the difference was not statistically significant (Appendix A4). 
0151 Data extraction  
0152 We extracted data from the title and abstract of each record that passed screening; full texts were not retrieved. Table 2 lists the fields for each arm. Bibliographic data came from CrossRef, OpenAlex and PubMed, without language models. We removed duplicate publications that shared a DOI, or a normalised title and publication year. 
0153 HF. Two language models from different developers assigned each study one or more financing functions, using a written codebook; a third settled their disagreements. Studies in which no function was found were reassessed by two further models and excluded only if neither found one. GLM-5.2 then extracted study design, data characteristics and the outcome domains each study addressed. 
0154 HEE. GLM-5.2 extracted six fields, each with a verbatim supporting quotation: type of economic evaluation, full-evaluation status, evidence base, use of a decision-analytic model, countries studied, and clinical consequences. A study was included only if it was a full economic evaluation with a clinical consequence. 
0155 In both arms, this step was the final eligibility check. We tested the extraction once on human-coded records not used in its development (Appendix A5–A7): 
0156 HF codes agreed with two reviewers’ coding on 90% of decisions (κ 0.46), more closely than the reviewers agreed with each other (87%; κ 0.30). 
0157 HEE eligibility decisions agreed with a reviewer’s on 88.5% of a blind sample of 160. The errors ran one way: about one accepted study in ten was not a full evaluation.

0181 Coding and linkage to external data 
0182 Countries studied were linked to World Bank regions and income groups using the current World Bank classification (World Bank 2026). For HEE, a language model coded the condition targeted by each intervention to a Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 cause, following IHME’s mapping of ICD-10 codes to GBD causes (Vos et al. 2020); 87.6% of studies received a cause. 0183 Analysis 
0184 Analyses are descriptive. We report counts and proportions with Wilson 95% confidence intervals where proportions are estimated from samples. A study can address several functions, countries, diseases or outcomes, so these counts can exceed the number of studies. Comparisons by income group use single-country studies. 
0185 For HEE, we compared each disease area’s share of studies with its share of disability-adjusted life-years, separately for high-income and for low- and middle-income countries. This follows a long line of research comparing research output with disease burden (Albarqouni et al. 2018; Do et al. 2021; Schmallenbach et al. 2025). The ratio is a reference point, not a target: research priorities also reflect tractability, intervention cost and existing evidence. We also describe authorship by the income group of the country studied and, for HF, the functions supported by the most frequent funders. 
0186 To describe themes beyond the coded fields, we grouped studies by the similarity of their titles and abstracts (sentence embeddings, UMAP and HDBSCAN; Reimers and Gurevych 2019). This gave 73 topic clusters for HEE and 56 for HF. Analyses were done in R (version 4.4.1) and Python. 
0187 Interactive evidence map and chatbot development 
0188 To share our research as a public good, we developed a web-based interactive evidence landscape map. The platform allows users to explore our data and findings, with filters to subset studies with specific characteristics, geographical focus, time horizons and more. The map is divided with separate platforms for HF and HEE, with each consisting of: (1) an overview presenting key findings and figures at glance; (2) a thematic gallery with data visitations presenting findings; (3) an interactive explorer that allows uses to filter records, examine cross-tabulations and access a table of individual publications; and (4) a country module with geographical evidence landscapes and individual country profiles. In addition, in the explorer, users can download csv files containing full or filtered study records. 
0189 Our platform exists on a static website written in HTML, CSS and JavaScript with interactive charts using Plotly.js. Python scrips were used to present the extracted variables and export them as JSON files. We did not require a server-side database, which was considered easier for hosting and maintenance requirements. 
0190 In the interactive map, we added a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)-based AI chatbot as an embedded feature to facilitate user queries and quick exploration of the evidence base. The chatbot supports users to answer questions about the volume of studies, and their characteristics and distribution. We defined its intended role, target audience, response guidelines and constraints, including how to handle questions that could not be answered. We developed a set of representative user questions and reference answers to support chatbot testing. We verified the chatbot performance through a two-stage process of LLM-assisted assessment, reviews by human reviewers, according to the pre-defined criteria that includes response relevance, completeness, and accuracy. 
0191 Results 
0192 Navigating One Million Records: From a Broad Search to a Focused Evidence Base  
0193 After removing duplicates, the search returned 1,154,050 records published between 2010 and 3 August 2026. We included 53,411 studies in the health financing (HF) arm and 41,610 publications in the health economic evaluation (HEE) arm (Figure 1). The arms were screened independently against different criteria, and 1,847 studies qualified for both. Each arm is reported on its own, and the two counts are not added together. 
0194 The findings describe the included literature as identified from titles and abstracts. On an independent sample screened by the review team, the final HF set recovered 68% of eligible studies and the HEE screening 86%. Financing functions, outcomes and evaluation characteristics were coded from abstracts. Income groups follow the current World Bank classification, except where we state that the group in the year of publication was used.

0198 Health financing  
0199 Research on health financing functions is large and growing fast 
0200 We included 53,411 health financing studies. Of these, 49,800 (93%) were empirical analyses while the remaining were literature reviews synthesizing evidence on focused health financing topics. The most common study designs were cross-sectional and correlational analyses (n=11,036, 21%), policy and implementation-based studies (n=8,284, 16%), experimental and quasi-experimental studies (n=7,090, 13%), and descriptive analyses of health systems (n=6,235, 12%). The field is growing fast: 29,190 studies (55%) were published in 2019 or later (supplementary figure X0). 
0201 Health financing research is concentrated in a few high-income countries, above all the United States 
0202 In all, 44,122 studies (83%) named at least one country, giving 47,488 study-country pairs across 198 countries and territories. Most studies examined a single country (79%), 14% had a multi-country, regional or global focus, and 6% named no place. The literature is concentrated: 29% of studies (15,487) were about one country, the United States. Relative to population, research is densest in North America, Australia, New Zealand and parts of northern and western Europe, and thinnest in North Africa and the Middle East, parts of Central Africa and Central Asia (Figure 2).

0204 Benefits decisions, purchasing and pooling dominate; but the mix differs between richer and poorer countries 
0205 Each study could be coded as addressing several financing functions. Studies were most commonly associated with benefits decisions (25% of studies), followed by purchasing (22%), pooling (20%), revenue raising (16%), public financial management (14%), studies with a generic health financing landscape-level focus (10%), recurrent financing for the procurement and distribution of supply chain inputs (9%), capital investment (8%), which itself covers infrastructure (4%) and other inputs. 
0206 The mix differs by the income status of the country studied (Figure 3). Studies of high-income countries concentrated on purchasing (28% of single-country studies, against 16% for low- and middle-income countries) and benefits decisions (28% against 21%). Studies of low- and middle-income countries more often examined revenue raising (19% against 10%), public financial management (19% against 13%), the financing system as a whole (14% against 7%) and supply-chain financing (13% against 5%). Pooling was studied relatively equally in both (22% and 23%). In low-income countries alone, revenue raising (22%) was studied almost as often as benefits decisions (23%), and supply-chain financing reached 16%.

0212 Each function covers a recognizable set of subjects 
0213 To describe what the studies within each function were about, we explored comparing the topics of each function’s studies with the topics of all studies (Figure 5). Topics came from grouping studies by the similarity of their titles and abstracts (56 topics; 25% of studies were not assigned to a topic).  
0214 The thematic profiles were distinct across financing functions. Revenue-raising studies were dominated by health taxes, particularly tobacco and sugar-sweetened beverage taxes, followed by development assistance, HIV financing and health-expenditure trends. Pooling research focused on risk selection in insurance markets, community-based health insurance and national insurance schemes, whereas purchasing research centred on provider-payment reforms, including accountable care organisations, diagnosis-related groups and performance-based financing. Studies of benefits decisions examined what services and products were covered and for whom, including fee exemptions, cancer and orphan drugs, and genetic and prenatal testing. Public financial management research addressed hospital management, municipal health budgets and budget-impact analyses of high-cost medicines. Supply-chain financing focused mainly on procurement of medicines, vaccines, biosimilars and malaria commodities, while capital-investment studies examined digital health systems, medical equipment, research funding and vaccine development. Studies of the financing system as a whole largely comprised analyses of national reforms across diverse country settings. 
0215 Several large topics corresponded to a single country’s financing system. United States public insurance (Medicaid and Medicare) accounted for 27% of single-country studies of high-income countries; national insurance programmes in China (19%), India (8%), Kenya and other African countries (5%) and Indonesia (4%) were the largest topics in studies of low- and middle-income countries. Medicines was the largest topic common to both (15% and 9%).

Among them, national governments were the largest group (52.5%), followed by universities, hospitals and institutes (22.4%) and foundations and non-profit organizations (21.7%) (Supplementary Figure S3).

North Africa and the Middle East (about 13% of aid and 2% of pairs), South Asia (11% and 7%) and Central Europe, Eastern Europe and Central Asia (4% and 1%) also accounted for a smaller share of research than of aid. 0236 Health economic evaluations 
0237 Health economic evaluations are numerous, mostly cost-utility analyses, and growing fast 
0238 Of 41,610 publications, 39,625 (95.2%) are original full economic evaluations (Figure 10). Most of these (38,958) are stand-alone evaluations, while the other 667 also conducted a review, for example, a systematic literature review followed by the authors’ own economic evaluation. The remaining 1,985 publications (4.8%) are reviews only that synthesized published evaluations. Among the 39,625 original economic evaluations, 48.2% are cost-utility analysis (CUA), 25.6% cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA), 22.8% cost-consequence analysis (CCA) with cost-benefit analysis (CBA) forming the remainder (3.4%). The production rate of recent health economic evidence is increasing: over the 16-year time horizon of the mapping, 51% of the included records were published in the last 6.5 years— since 2020 (Supplementary Figure X0).

0242 Health economic evidence is concentrated in a few countries 
0243 Most evaluations (71.9%) named one or more countries as the focus or setting of the research. One hundred eighty-seven of the world’s 204 countries or territories were featured in at least one economic evaluation (Figure 10). The evidence is highly concentrated in specific countries (Gini coefficient = 0.84). The United States, the United Kingdom and China account for 49.7% of the studies that name a country, the top five (adding Canada and the Netherlands) for 57.7%, and the top ten for 70.7% (adding Australia, Spain, Japan, Germany and Italy). 0244 Cancer and cardiovascular disease dominate, and pharmaceuticals are the most evaluated interventions 
0245 A GBD 2023 cause (IHME 2025) was assigned to 36,446 evaluations (87.6%): 34,753 to one of the 22 level-2 causes and 1,693 to an infection or injury without a specific cause. The remaining evaluations concerned no specific disease (2,500), a risk factor only (1,516) or a condition that cannot be attributed to a single cause (1,070). Evaluations most often concerned cancers (7,856 evaluations), with research spread evenly across income settings. 0265 Research on low- and middle-income countries is less often led locally 
0266 Funding sources could not be extracted from around three out of every four studies (n=30,508). Among those where data on funding could be obtained, 18% of studies indicated funding from the United States National Institutes for Health. The National Science Foundation of China was the next most credited funder (4%), followed by the Gates Foundation (3%), the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) of Australia (3%), and Pfizer (2%). NIH funding tended to focus study investigation in HICs, while the Gates and NSFC funding was targeted to L&MICs (Supplementary Figure X3). 
0267 Overall, 66.7% of studies with known “open-access” status were free to read as a global good (n=24,216). Within studies that reported at least one country with an identified income group, 70.4% were open access. Among them, 81.9% of studies focused on LICs or LMICs were open access, compared to 69.4% in UMICs or HICs (Supplementary Figure X4). Among the subset of studies focused on one country from which author institutional affiliation could be extracted, most had an author based in that geography. However, only 67% of studies assessing L&MICs had at least one author affiliated with an institution based in L&MICs, compared to 96% of HICs (Supplementary Figure X5). 
0268 Research falls short of disease burden in lower-income countries, and for injuries, mental health and cardiovascular disease 
0269 There is no agreed-upon metric for determining what constitutes sufficient research. However, one strand of literature has sought to provide insight by examining whether a country’s or disease’s share of global research aligns with its share of the burden of disease—measured using disability adjusted life years (DALYs). Building directly on this literature, we matched estimates of the burden of disease to research output, by disease and geography. Studies assessing more than one country or disease were counted once for each. 
0270 Among 154 countries with a population greater than one million (31,437 study-country counts), lower-income countries tend to have less health economic evidence relative to their disease burden (supplementary figure X). Low-income countries had around ten times less literature than might be expected, shrinking to 4.4 and 2.4 times less in lower-middle and upper-middle income countries respectively. High income countries had 2.3 times more literature relative to their burden share.  
0271 Several diseases remain relatively understudied relative to the health burden that they impose (Figure 15). Across all income settings, health economic evidence for interventions that can cost-effectively reduce injuries is scarce. Interventions targeting mental and substance use disorders and cardiovascular disease are also relatively understudied considering the burden they impose. In lower income settings, health economic evidence does not proportionately target maternal and neonatal health, diarrheal infections, and skin disorders.

0275 Discussion 
0276 Main findings 
0277 This map brings together 53,411 studies of health financing functions and 41,610 full economic evaluations published since 2010. Both literatures are large and growing. Around half of the studies in each arm were published in 2020 or later. Across both topics the literature is concentrated in a few high-income countries. The United States alone accounts for 29 per cent of health financing studies, and the United States, the United Kingdom and China together account for half of the economic evaluations.  
0278 Health financing research is dominated by benefits decisions, purchasing and pooling. Capital investment (8% of studies) and the financing of supply chains (9.5%) are rarely studied, although both are among the fastest-growing areas, with more than half of studies published since 2020 (57% of supply-chain financing studies and 52% of capital investment). Economic evaluations are dominated by cancer and cardiovascular disease, and a third evaluate pharmaceuticals. 
0279 Research themes are aligned with national systems. In HICs, United States public insurance (Medicaid and Medicare) accounts for over a quarter of single-country health financing studies, while national insurance programmes in China, India, Kenya and Indonesia are the largest topics in L&MICs. Among economic evaluations, the topics most studied in HICs, such as dementia, cancer medicines and knee replacement surgeries, receive little attention in L&MICs. But apart from malaria, dengue and tuberculosis, the leading topics in L&MICs are also well studied in HICs, so the reverse does not hold. 
0280 Research output is lowest relative to need in the poorest countries 
0281 Half a century ago, Tudor Hart (1971) observed that the availability of good medical care tends to vary inversely with the need for it in the population served. Our analysis shows the same pattern for research. The countries that spend least on health and depend most on aid have the least research, relative to their population and disease burden.  
0282 For example, low-income countries are the focus of 3% of health financing studies, and the median low-income country has 6.4 economic evaluations per 10 million people, against 73.4 in the median high-income country. Relative to their share of the global disease burden, low- and lower-middle-income countries have less than a fifth of the economic evidence than what a proportionate share would give them (ratios 0.18 and 0.15), while HICs have more than four times theirs (4.3). Sub-Saharan Africa, which received about half of development assistance for health in 2022–24 (53%), only accounts around a tenth of the countries examined in health financing studies. These are the countries now facing cuts in development assistance for health (IHME 2025a), and with them the most urgent decisions about how to raise and spend domestic resources. However, they have the most limited evidence base to draw on in making those decisions. 
0283 Despite recent attention to nationally led research and equitable partnerships, the data on research authorship is a stark reminder of a still prevailing inequity. The share of studies with no author based in the country studied rises steadily down the income scale, from very few for HICs, to around one in five for lower-middle-income countries and almost a third for low-income countries. Research written by authors from the country of research only is the norm for HICs (80%; 62%), but rare for low-income countries (19%; 5%). Most research on low-income countries is co-authored by national and foreign researchers (50%; 63%).  
0284 Where research is produced matters for both research quality and impact. In particular, financing reforms depend on domestic institutions, fiscal rules and political settlements. And the Lancet Global Health Commission on High-Quality Health Systems suggest country ownership, domestic institutional capacity and the embedding of evidence in national decisions as features of a learning health system (Kruk and Pate 2020). Research on health financing and accountability in low-income countries that are led from abroad is less likely to build that capacity. 
0285 A key feature of the evidence base is the lack of reliable information on funding sources. Most studies do not report a funder, making the corresponding analysis more difficult to interpret. Among health financing studies that do name one, national governments are the largest group (52.5%). A review of funding opportunities for health policy and systems research found that international organizations and bilateral agencies were the main international funders, and that their funding was often tied to specific large interventions (Kentikelenis et al. 2023). Without better reporting, funders cannot see how their combined investments are distributed or where they overlap.  
0286 Making new research count: target the gaps and design for synthesis 
0287 Researchers and funders can make each new study more useful in two ways: by directing it to the gaps where evidence is scarcest relative to the need, and by ensuring it is designed and reported so that it can be combined with what already exists. We discuss both in turn. 
0288 Our mapping of the literature highlights several specific gaps. The ratio of research to disease burden (each cause’s share of studies divided by its share of DALYs) is a reference point, not a target. Research priorities reflect a combination of different factors, including routes to institutional uptake, feasibility and intervention cost, as well as what evidence already exists. With that caveat, four groups of gaps stand out. 
0289 Country income and Geographical gaps: Low- and lower-middle-income countries, and Sub-Saharan Africa in particular, are underrepresented in the health financing and health economics literature. Absolute and relative measures point to different countries. The largest absolute gaps against a proportionate share of burden are in India, China and Nigeria, while the largest relative gaps are in North Korea, Angola and Yemen.  
0290 Economic evidence by disease and injury: Economic evidence on injuries is scarce in every setting: transport injuries have a research-to-burden ratio of 0.02 in L&MICs and 0.01 in HICs, and self-harm and violence 0.07 and 0.11. Mental health conditions (0.36 in L&MICs; GBD ‘mental disorders’), cardiovascular diseases (0.53), neurological disorders (0.51) and musculoskeletal disorders (0.50) are also below a proportionate share across L&MICs and HICs. Maternal and neonatal disorders (0.54), skin diseases (0.68) and enteric infections (0.80) are understudied in L&MICs specifically. 
0291 Financing evidence by function and outcome: Capital investment and supply-chain financing are the least studied functions. Only 5% of public financial management studies examine transparency and accountability, although the Lancet Global Health Commission on Financing Primary Health Care treats public financial management as central to getting money to the frontline (Hanson et al. 2022). Responsiveness is examined in 4% of purchasing studies. Fewer than a quarter of health financing studies examine effects on health, and most focus on coverage and spending. 
0292 Type of research design: Health financing research is mostly observational, with cross-sectional and correlational analyses (21%) being the most common design. Experimental and quasi-experimental evaluations make up 13% of single-country studies of L&MICs. We did not assess rigour, but more impact evaluations of financing reforms in L&MICs would make the evidence more useful for decisions. 
0293 How a study is designed and reported determines how it adds to what is known. Avoidable duplication and poor reporting are well-documented sources of research waste (Chalmers and Glasziou 2009). New research should start with the existing evidence, and use measures and reporting that let others build on it.  
0294 Generic health measures such as DALYs and QALYs are the best examples of this, allowing results to be compared across diseases and settings. We find relatively low uptake of both outcome measures. QALYs are reported in 54% of evaluations from HICs and 40% of evaluations from L&MICs, and DALYs in 12% of evaluations of L&MICs.  
0295 Health economics has established reporting guidelines, of which the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) are the most widely used (Husereau et al. 2022), but only a minority of studies follow them (Maurya and Munshi 2023; Caulley et al. 2020). Funders can make these practices conditions of their grants, requiring researchers to show how the study builds on existing evidence, report generic health measures, follow CHEERS and other relevant guidelines, state the setting, perspective and threshold in the abstract, and acknowledge the funder. 
0296 The same practices would make knowledge easier to accumulate and keep current with AI. A key limitation of this systematic map is the information available in the study record and corresponding meta-data. Standardized, machine-readable reporting of study-level results, together with open sharing of study data, would allow future synthesis efforts to more easily extract effect sizes and cost-effectiveness ratios directly, link publications to studies, and stay up to date as new research appears.  
0297 Large evidence base ready for synthesis 
0298 The gaps in 4.2 and 4.3 sit alongside a large body of research that has not yet been synthesized. The map includes more than 95,000 studies. Economic evaluations cover 157 of the 160 countries with more than one million people, and L&MICs account for 41% of evaluations of vaccines and 32% of evaluations of financing and payment policies. The health financing arm includes more than 7,000 experimental and quasi-experimental studies and 1,357 studies of low-income countries.  
0299 The map shows where synthesis could answer policy questions now. Pooling studies concentrate on equitable distribution (32%) and financial protection (19%), and national insurance programmes in China, India, Kenya, Indonesia and other countries form some of the largest topic clusters in L&MICs, which makes the effects of insurance and pooling reforms on financial protection an obvious candidate for systematic review. Public financial management studies focus on efficiency (36%) and health (20%). Among economic evaluations, nearly half of which report QALYs or DALYs, there is enough evidence to compare the cost-effectiveness of interventions across conditions within settings, and to test how far results transfer between settings, for vaccines, tuberculosis, malaria and HPV among others.  
0300 For funders that increasingly allocate by expected cost-effectiveness, synthesis of this kind can inform decisions across many countries at a fraction of the cost of new primary studies, and it identifies where new primary research would add most. 
0301 The map and its data are openly available through an interactive platform, with downloadable study records and a chatbot for querying the evidence. Kept up to date, it can serve as a starting point for these syntheses and as a way to track whether new research moves towards the gaps identified here. 
0302 Conclusions 
0303 Using Large Language Models (LLM) and adopting replicable methods, mapping the global evidence on health systems financing was feasible in a short timeline. The evidence base is large, but funders and decision-makers should avoid wasting resources and tailor efforts towards need-based research that will contribute to enhancing the health system financing, building on evidence from economic evaluation. Making this map living will ensure its relevance for a longer term, and can strategically prioritize future investments.  
0304 Limitations 
0305 The map was limited to publications since 2010, so apparent gaps may overlook evidence produced earlier. 
0306 Grey literature was excluded, which may underrepresent evidence produced by governments, international organizations, and other non-journal sources. 
0307 Search terms were developed in English; although no language restriction was planned for inclusion, this may underrepresent literature using non-English terminology. 
0308 Health financing terminology is heterogeneous, so keyword searching may have missed relevant studies. 
0309 The review prioritized broad mapping rather than study-level quality/risk-of-bias appraisal or synthesis of effect sizes. 
0310 Machine-learning and LLM-assisted screening/classification/extraction can introduce classification error; report empirical validation results and the residual uncertainty relevant to interpretation. 
0311 Retraction checks cannot identify all fabricated, manipulated, or otherwise unreliable studies that have not been formally retracted. 
0312 The evidence shows partnership among authors from different income levels are common, however the data can't show whether these are equitable partnerships or the nature of the arrangements and the roles played by each partner.
